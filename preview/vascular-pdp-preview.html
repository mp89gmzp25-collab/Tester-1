Good numbers. Cold hands.

Your last bloodwork came back fine. The doctor said keep doing what you're doing. And you still can't feel your feet by the evening, your hands go cold at your desk, and the stairs take something out of you they didn't used to.

A blood test is an inventory. It counts what's floating in your blood on the morning they draw it. It has never once measured how the blood moves — how easily it gets to your hands, your feet, the small vessels at the ends of you.

That's the gap. Good numbers on paper. Cold hands in the room. This page is about the second one.

Your body builds and clears all day. After fifty, one side slows down.

  1. You build small repairs constantly. Every time a vessel takes a little wear, your body patches it. This is normal. It's supposed to happen.
  2. Then a clearing system breaks those patches back down so the blood keeps moving freely. Build, clear, build, clear — all day, without you noticing.
  3. After about fifty, the clearing side slows. The building side doesn't. [CITATION NEEDED — fibrinolytic decline with age]
  4. So you build like you're 25 and clear like you're 65. Same repairs going up. Fewer coming back down.
  5. Here's the part nobody mentions. Diet, exercise, and almost every bottle on the counter are aimed at building less. Almost nothing is aimed at clearing more. That's the side that slowed down.
That's why this is a different conversation from your statin or your fish oil. Those work on the building side. This supports your body's normal fibrinolytic activity — the clearing side — so blood keeps moving the way it did when the clearing kept up. [CITATION NEEDED — nattokinase fibrinolytic mechanism]

The dose is the whole argument.

FU stands for fibrinolytic units. It's not the weight of the capsule — it's how much clearing activity is actually in a serving. Two bottles can hold the same milligrams and deliver completely different FU. FU is the number that matters. Everything else on the label is packaging.

Here's the part the ads got right: below a real dose, the research doesn't apply to what you're holding. Most bottles sell at 2,000 to 5,000 FU per serving. That's not a smaller version of the same thing. It's under the line where the evidence lives.

About that one study that found nothing. Someone has probably pointed you at a trial that ran for years, on a few hundred people, and found no effect. Read the method. That trial dosed at roughly 2,000 FU. It didn't test a real dose and come up empty. It tested a fifth of one.
